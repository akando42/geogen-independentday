# Yellow Yangtze Easily Impregnable Gene Groups

The delta region between the Yellow River and the Yangtze—from Xi’an to Qingdao, and from Beijing to Nanjing—is the largest flatland area in East Asia. It is highly fertile for agricultural development and equally favorable for infrastructure construction.

The earliest Tibetan–East Asian walled cities, road networks, canals, and writing systems were established in this Yellow–Yangtze food basket.

To this day, the Yellow–Yangtze gene groups continue to dominate critical sectors of modern China, including information, finance, and energy. Major corporations such as Baidu, Xiaomi, Foxconn, Wanda, ByteDance, ICBC, China Merchants Bank, Sinopec, and JD.com maintain their headquarters in Beijing and surrounding areas.

## Western Flank: The Eternal Mountain Wall

On the western flank lies the Chengdu “Eternal Mountain” Great Wall of China, stretching from the Gobi Desert in the north to the Tibetan Plateau in the south. This Eternal Mountain system forms a natural fortress, shielding the Yellow–Yangtze food basket from western invasions while also limiting expansionary Chinese dynasties from reaching Central Asia, Pakistan, or India through this treacherous terrain.
Eastern Flank: The Long Coastline

To the east, China possesses an exceptionally long coastline, from the ports of Guangzhou and Shenzhen in the south to Dalian in the north. Scattered along this vast coast are near-shore islands that have historically functioned both as enemy naval bases and as Chinese forward guard posts: Hainan, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Okinawa, and Japan itself have all shifted hands between the Yin–Yang farming kingdoms, the Qing dynasty, the British Empire, the Japanese Empire, and more recently, the United States.

The calm seas between Qingdao and Japan enabled centuries of trade as well as naval invasions among Northeast Asian civilizations—Mongolia, Manchuria, Korea, and Japan. The Yellow Sea near Dalian and the Sea of Japan near Korea became hubs of relentless commerce and the sites of major naval battles: the Opium Wars between Britain and Qing China, the Russo–Japanese War of 1904–05, and the Sino–Japanese battles of World War II.

## Northern Gap: A Weak Point in the Wall

Although the Eternal Mountain Range protected much of the northern flank of the Yellow–Yangtze basin, it left a small gap near Beijing. This opening allowed successive foreign gene groups—the Mongols, the Manchus, and later the Japanese Empire—to penetrate deep into the Yellow–Yangtze interior, leaving a long-lasting imprint on the genetic composition of China.

Beyond this northern shield lies Siberia, a vast and underdeveloped region. Only with the Trans-Siberian Railway did the Russian Empire succeed in establishing permanent settlements in this harsh land. Surviving its six-month winters required constant resupply of food, fuel, and manpower from Moscow’s core population centers.

## Western Expansion and Central Asia

Beyond the Eternal Mountains in the west lay regions historically outside China’s sphere. However, with the aid of Soviet fuel and trucks in the 1940s, and later the Chinese Silk Rail Network in the 2000s, Yellow–Yangtze gene groups have been able to establish settlements closer to the Central Asian steppe.
Yet, Soviet projects of the 1980s that drained lakes and rivers left Central Asia with severe water shortages, restricting population growth. This scarcity has prevented both Yellow–Yangtze gene groups and Caspian steppe groups from sustaining large human settlements there.

## Southern Regions and Vietic Tribes

South of the Yellow–Yangtze lies a mountainous zone historically governed by hundreds of Viet tribes. Within this southern region, three small fertile basins—Wuhan, Nanchang, and Shenzhen—enabled large-scale settlement and agriculture.

Nanchang became the base where Mao Zedong (“MAD Mao Trach Dong”) launched his Communist movement. Shenzhen later became the site where Deng Xiaoping (“DAM Dang Tieu Binh”) began his modernization drive.

Further south, a shielded ocean along the coasts of Vietnam and the Philippines facilitated centuries of trade between Yellow–Yangtze ports in the north and Mekong ports in the south, driven by monsoon winds. Winter winds carried Chinese dynasties southward to invade the Vietnamese coast, while summer reversals enabled Vietnamese traders and migrants to sail northward to Canton and Shanghai—leaving enduring genetic footprints in Chinese coastal towns.

The Southern Mountain Range largely blocked Yellow–Yangtze gene groups from moving southward by land, allowing independent gene pools to thrive in the Dali–Kunming highlands and the Red River delta. Only with Mongolian horses in the 13th century, gasoline trucks in the 19th, and electromagnetic rail in the 21st have Yellow–Yangtze powers managed to breach this barrier and connect directly with Mekong food-basket gene groups by land.

## Agriculture and State Formation

The origins of Chinese civilization rest on the invention of wet-rice agriculture at Zhengzhou. This technology spread throughout Yellow–Yangtze kingdoms, enabling food surpluses, fortified cities, and standing armies.

During the Three Kingdoms period, western mountain groups, northeastern nomads, and southern naval tribes contested the food basket, dividing it into three “gene capitals”: Xi’an, Beijing, and Nanjing. These centers struggled for dominance until the eventual victory of the southern naval tribes based in Wuhan.
From that victory emerged the Han writing system, which spread across the Yellow–Yangtze basin. For centuries afterward—up until the Qing dynasty—the region was primarily ruled by southern naval groups, with capitals alternating between Wuhan and Nanjing. Their rule was interrupted first by the Mongol invasion through the Beijing gap in the 13th century, and finally subdued by Manchurian horsemen in the 17th.

## 20th Century Upheavals

During World War II, Japanese air and naval supremacy devastated Beijing and Nanjing, producing countless widows and illegitimate children in China, Hainan, and Taiwan. The Japanese advance was halted at Zhengzhou, sparing Xi’an.

The war drastically reshaped China’s gene distribution. Mountainous groups from Xi’an, Wuhan, and Nanchang endured, while many Manchurian nomadic lines were destroyed either in wartime or later by the Red Army.
Mao Zedong’s Cultural Revolution relocated coastal populations inland, while importing new gene groups from Switzerland, Kazakhstan, Korea, and Japan. These imported groups became the genetic foundation of modern industrialized coastal cities like Qingdao and Shanghai. They also gained disproportionate political influence within the Communist Party Congress, controlling resource allocation and directing Soviet-style apartment development nationwide.

After the collapse of the Soviet Union, Deng Xiaoping’s modernization invited massive foreign direct investment from Japan, Korea, and California. This transformed China with new factories, roads, dams, and railways, while introducing new genetic inputs from those regions. Population control also became a weapon: HIV and food-based endocrine disruptors were deployed against older gene groups, slowing their growth under the pressures of consumer capitalism.

## Surveillance and Genetic Control

By 2020, advanced surveillance systems—including facial recognition cameras from Japanese-funded firms in Nanjing such as Hikvision and Dahua—were deployed across both the Yellow–Yangtze and Mekong basins. These systems were allegedly linked to campaigns spreading HIV and STD-related diseases among older native groups, while Yellow–Yangtze women were targeted through smartphones to increase the likelihood of pregnancies with Japanese, Korean, Kazakh, or Swiss paternal lines arriving via Vietnam, Taiwan, and California.
